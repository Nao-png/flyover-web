# How it works

[日本語](how-it-works.md) | English

This page covers what doesn't fit in the [README](../README.en.md): how the data is fetched and how the globe viewer is built.

## Fetching the data

1. Fetch the **resource manifest** (protobuf) to get the URL of the Flyover tiles (style 15) and the `tokenP2` used for authentication
2. From the **list of height regions** named in the manifest (`altitude-*.xml`), pick the region containing the point. A region's name `Reg_z9_X_Y` is the zoom 9 tile it covers
3. Request tiles by `x, y, z, h`. The URL carries `sid` and `accessKey` (built with AES-256-CBC)
4. Read the **C3M** (the 3D model of one tile). The mesh is compressed with Apple's own variant of Edgebreaker (Huffman codes → CLERS string → corner table → parallelogram prediction to restore vertices and UVs). Images are HEIC
5. Convert Earth-centered coordinates (ECEF) to east/north/up in meters, convert HEIC to JPEG, and export for the browser

Satellite imagery is style 7, and terrain is style 17 (zoom 7 and 11 worldwide, 13 for the US and Europe). The versions are in the style settings of the manifest.

## Globe viewer

### Choosing tiles

- Flyover tiles exist from zoom 13 (about 4 km per tile) to 20 (about 30 m). The viewer (CesiumJS) walks the quadtree down from the zoom 9 regions and picks finer tiles where they look large on screen (over 520 device pixels)
- It loads only every other level on the way down (about 40% less to load)
- It refines quarter by quarter where descendants can fill the quarter. Where they can't yet, or where children have no data (water and such), it draws the parent clipped to that quarter
- Until the needed tiles arrive, the gap is filled with finer tiles already at hand (up to 3 levels down) or coarser ones, so the 3D doesn't disappear while you move

### Loading order

- Up to 48 tiles load at once. More doesn't help, because the network link is already full (on a 50–65 Mbps line, 48 requests reached the limit; 96 only made each tile wait longer)
- Tiles visible on screen load first, the ones that look largest first. Off-screen tiles around the camera (2.5× the altitude, 4× that for coarse tiles) are preloaded only after all visible tiles have arrived, so they're ready when you rotate

### Lightening the browser's load

- Nothing is drawn while the view is still (Cesium's requestRenderMode). It draws only when the camera moves, or while something is loading or animating
- While loading, the heavy parts are preparing shaders (translating them for the GPU) and sending images to the GPU. Flyover models are unlit (the photo as is), so Cesium's reflections (the dynamic environment map and IBL) and the preparation for `Scene#pick` are turned off. Once the reflections became ready, they made Cesium rebuild every shader, which ate the first few seconds of loading (74 shader programs → 53)
- The cursor elevation and scale in the bottom bar are only rechecked once a second when neither the camera nor the mouse has moved (reading the depth stalls the GPU)
- The time the globe spends on loading each frame is raised from 5 ms to 20 ms

### Conversion on the server

- The height slabs of a tile are packed into one glb (vertices in Earth-centered coordinates) and kept in `cache/glb4/`
- Vertex positions are packed as int16 and UVs as uint16 (KHR_mesh_quantization; 8 cm steps even on a 4.9 km tile)
- When the browser can decode HEVC with the GPU's dedicated hardware (WebCodecs; Chrome doesn't decode HEVC in software), images of the new Flyover are passed as HEVC instead of JPEG (`?hevc=1`, `cache/glb98_16h/`). A HEIC holds just one HEVC frame, so its configuration (hvcC) and the frame go into the material's `extras.hevc`. The viewer decodes them in a worker (`earth/hevc.js`), uses the luma (Y) and chroma (UV) planes directly as textures of a Cesium CustomShader, and converts to RGB in the shader. HEIC carries no color description, so it is treated as BT.601 full range as the HEIF rules say (the same as pillow_heif; Chrome otherwise assumes BT.709 limited range)
  - Building a glb on the server drops from about 125 ms to 19 ms per height slab (no HEIC decoding, shrinking or JPEG). Decoding in the browser takes about 10 ms per image
  - Images stay at their original size (in WebGL2, textures made from arrays are not enlarged to powers of two) at 1.5 bytes per pixel. With 208 tiles loaded over Osaka, images take 438 MB of GPU memory (754 MB as power-of-two JPEG, 2,885 MB before shrinking)
  - Reading a decoded frame back to the CPU (`VideoFrame#copyTo`) took about 100 ms per image and was the heaviest step. So frames are now received as RGB images that stay on the GPU (ImageBitmap) and turned back into BT.601 full-range Y and UV on the GPU, as textures of the same size and format (drawing into a single-channel R8 texture and generating mipmaps took seconds per image on this PC, so they are drawn into RGBA and copied into LUMINANCE and LUMINANCE_ALPHA). The decoder (VideoDecoder) is also reused instead of being recreated. Loading 107 tiles at Tokyo Station went from about 6.5 s to 3.7 s with the same GPU memory, and colors differ by about 1 level on average. With seasonal colors (`?season=`) the photo colors are read on the CPU, so frames are read back. `?gpu=0` always reads them back
  - Which material's image to use is told by the vertex attribute `_SLAB` (the 4th int16 of the position, which used to be padding). If decoding fails, JPEG is used from then on. Add `?hevc=0` to use JPEG only
- Images are shrunk so that width and height are each the next lower power of two. Cesium enlarges images that are not powers of two to the next power of two before building the downscaled levels (mipmaps), so the 1152–1376 px images common in the new Flyover became 2048 px, about 2.5 times the GPU memory (for 314 tiles loaded over Osaka, images are estimated to drop from 3.6 GB to about 1.0 GB)
- Tiles at the edge of Flyover coverage contain a gray band that joins the flat map (mesh kind 4) and a plane at height 0 (kind 3). On top of the terrain these look like a second layer, so they are left out
- Height slabs split heights relative to the tile size. At zoom 16 and below there is only h = 0 (even for Tokyo Skytree or Denver), so zoom 15 and below request only h = 0, and zoom 16 only h = 0–1
- If numba is available, the heavy parts of C3M decoding (`flyover/_fast.py`) are compiled for speed (0.3 s → 0.06 s per tile in a dense city; the output is bit-for-bit identical to the pure Python version). Decoding runs in parallel in worker processes started at launch. So as not to compete with the browser for CPU, it uses a quarter of the cores (at most 4; each takes 140–190 MB of memory) at slightly lowered priority

### Requests to Apple

- They use HTTP/2 (httpx), with up to 90 concurrent requests on each of 4 connections. Apple takes about 1 second per tile, but the more you run in parallel, the faster it goes (with requests over HTTP/1.1, TLS setup per connection and so on meant waiting about 4 seconds for a response at 32 concurrent requests)
- If a request hasn't returned after 1.5 seconds, the same request goes out again on another connection and whichever returns first is used (and a third one 3 seconds later if still waiting)
- Each request gives up after 10 seconds. A connection that has stalled as a whole (nothing returned for a while) is reopened (before this, some requests waited 60 seconds)
- Terrain and satellite imagery are fetched on a connection separate from the Flyover tiles. While 16 or more Flyover tiles are being fetched, however, they are limited to 4 at a time. The link fills up either way, and much of the ground imagery is hidden under the 3D city, so doing the city first makes the view look good sooner

### Terrain

- Terrain is a 16-bit PNG. The 12 bytes appended after the end of the PNG (IEND) hold a float32 base height and scale (0.25): height = base + value × scale
- Heights are ellipsoidal, the same as Flyover, and the base differs per tile (miss it and inland terrain ends up hundreds of meters too low)
- The server converts the terrain into Cesium's geographic tiles
- EGM96 geoid heights (2.7 MB fetched from the PROJ data files on first run) are used for sea level where there is no terrain tile, and for the elevation shown on screen

### Other

- To get around the browser's limit on concurrent connections (6 per host), Flyover, satellite and terrain tiles are spread across different loopback addresses (127.0.0.2–127.0.0.17)
- Place search is relayed to OpenStreetMap's Nominatim

## What had changed on the servers by 2026

Since the original, retroplasma/flyover-reverse-engineering (archived in 2021), the following had changed. This project is adapted to them.

- The manifest's file location (`cache_base_url`) is now empty → use `https://gspe21-ssl.ls.apple.com/`
- The per-region index C3MM version 1 (style 14) now returns 404 (version 2, fetched by tile coordinates as style 52, does respond, but its format is not decoded) → skip the index, request the area's tiles and height slabs directly, and skip empty responses (the new Flyover has a separate index, style 100, which is decoded and used; see "Index" below)
- The 5th byte of C3M (the version) went from 3 to 7 → it reads fine with the version 3 reader
- Images changed from JPEG to HEIC (image format 13)

Also, the original tool picked the region "whose center is closest", which picks the wrong one where regions overlap, as in Tokyo (Shibuya returned no tiles at all). This project picks regions by the tile number in the region name `Reg_z9_X_Y`.

The authentication steps and `tokenP1` are the same as the Look Around implementation in [sk-zk/streetlevel](https://github.com/sk-zk/streetlevel) (which also comes from retroplasma). retroplasma's setup downloads the Xcode simulator SDK (about 2 GB) to extract `tokenP1`; that isn't needed here.

## The new Flyover (style 98)

The manifest requested as iOS 27 lists the new Flyover tiles (style 98). Start with `python scripts/earth.py --r3d` to use them at every zoom; only tiles without the new Flyover or that can't be read are filled in with the old C3M (so the scene doesn't switch between old and new data as you zoom). The reader is `flyover/r3d.py`.

- **Fetching**: Tile numbering and height slabs are the same as C3M. Request `?style=98&v=<style 98 version>&region=<region>&x&y&z&h` (`v` is the style 98 version, not the region's version)
- **Gaps in height slabs**: Unlike C3M, some tiles skip 4–7 above h = 0–3 and continue at 8–15. At Azabudai, 0–3 reach about 150 m above the ground, 4–7 are empty, and 8–15 go up to about 300 m (the upper halves of Mori JP Tower and Tokyo Tower; Tokyo Tower's upper lattice is mostly splats). Some tiles have 0–3 all empty and data only at 12, 14 and 15. Nothing was found above 15. Many other tiles do continue at 4–7 (no local tile had data in both 4–7 and 8–15). So at zoom 17 and above, if h = 3 has data or 0–3 are all empty, 4–15 are requested together, and the next 4 are requested while the last one has data (`fetch_heights` in `flyover/earth.py`; C3M still goes 4 at a time). Before, fetching stopped at the empty 4–7 and everything above 120–150 m was missing
- **File**: A container starting with `3D 96 59 2E` holds triangle splats (part 1) and a textured mesh (part 2). The mesh coordinates become Earth-centered coordinates (ECEF) with the 3×3 rotation and translation in the first entry of part 2. Textures are the image entries of part 2 (entry 3); a tile with several textures has that many entry 3s (3,317 of the 36,695 cached tiles, mostly places such as Fukuoka where the old Flyover's shapes were carried over). The first HEIC in each is the texture
- **Splats**: Trees and the like are triangle splats, not mesh (a park tile has a small mesh and close to 20,000 splats). Each has 3 vertices, a color and opacity, and 4 more values (see "The 4 splat values" below). Apple draws them denser toward the inside of the triangle, fading out at the edges. In the glb they are semi-transparent triangles with vertex colors (RGBA), with a shared small image that gets more transparent toward the edges, for a similar look (it doesn't match Apple's drawing pixel for pixel). Because they are semi-transparent, they are drawn from lowest to highest (unless drawn back to front, near colors get painted over by far ones and look muddy; the view is usually from above, so height order is enough)
- **Matching the splat look**: compared with 3 screenshots of Yumeshima (the forest inside the Grand Ring) from Maps on iOS 27. Feature matches between the screenshot and the export gave Apple's camera position and direction (PnP; the vertical field of view is 30° in every screenshot, median error 0.5 px), the same view was drawn, and brightness was correlated over the trees. Unsorted: 0.83–0.87. Lowest first: 0.91–0.95 (the same as re-sorting back to front for each view). Of edge-softness exponents 0.25–3, 1.5 is closest (0.95–0.97); using splat value 1 as a per-splat exponent is slightly further than a uniform 1.5 at every scale
- **Compression**: The content is the same Edgebreaker variant as C3M, split into streams and packed. Each number sequence is packed per "lane" with a bitmap (1 for the next value, 0 to repeat the previous value or look up a table) and Huffman codes (Apple's own table format)
  - **Shape**: The C / other bit per triangle and the 2-bit kind of the others are rebuilt into the old C3M single bit stream, and `edgebreaker.py` turns it into triangles
  - **Positions**: An integer per vertex × a step. It holds the difference from a parallelogram prediction while walking the triangles breadth first
  - **UV**: 4 bits per triangle mark UV seams. UV numbers are assigned depth first, bounded by seams, and the values are restored with a breadth-first parallelogram prediction bounded by seams. The unit is a quarter texel
  - **Lane directory (J)**: Bits for the lanes in use, a 2-bit kind per lane (a 2m + 6 bit field for m lanes), and a length and fill mark per lane. The kind says what changes for the next lane: if its high bit is 1, the next lane's length is written (otherwise it is the same); if its low bit is 1, 1 byte with the next lane's fill mark is written (otherwise the fill is the same). The first lane's mark sits at the byte boundary after the kind bits (when m leaves a remainder of 1 when divided by 4, it doesn't fit in the field and there is no mark). Marks: 0 = raw bytes, 1 and 4 = repeat, 2 and 5 = table lookup, 3 and 6 = signed differences. 1–3 are lanes of 8-bit values and 4–6 lanes of 16-bit values (true for 4,320 of 4,323 local lanes; the rest are 16-bit lanes whose values happen to be 255 or less)
  - **Splats**: BYET blocks for the position (XYZ) and 8 attributes. The origin is 4 bytes per component, or a packed form of that. One packed form is itself a small BYET (the bytes of the int as lanes, the components as values)
  - **Normals**: Spherical coordinates in 2° steps (not read now, since the view has no lighting)
- **Speed**:
  - The heavy loops (Huffman codes, lane filling, the 3 walks, prediction replay) run in numba. One tile takes 20–150 ms, 5–13 times faster than plain Python.
  - Reading the Huffman code tables and expanding the triangle connectivity (CLERS) also run in numba. Reading one height slab of the new Flyover went from about 36 ms to 23 ms (on 4,838 local tiles the output is exactly the same as before).
  - Each Huffman envelope is one numba call (read the table, check its shape, build the lookup table, read the body). The body is read by keeping a bit buffer and peeking a code length at a time, instead of one bit at a time. Splat decoding went from about 3.6 ms to 2.1 ms per tile, and reading one height slab from about 11.5 ms to 8.5 ms (on 1,500 local tiles the output is exactly the same as before).
  - So that broken data doesn't crash or hang the process, index ranges are checked before handing data to numba, the corner-table walks run with index checks (`boundscheck`), and loops have an upper limit.
  - In building one tile, decoding the image (HEIC, 100–150 ms) is the heaviest part.
  - The new Flyover is often not yet cached on Apple's delivery servers, so the first fetch of a tile takes 1–2 seconds to respond (tens of ms from the second time; it is also kept locally in `cache/r3d/`).
  - New Flyover tiles at z16 and below are large, around 1 MB each, so in a new place the distant view takes 1–2 seconds to appear. Tiles whose fetch times out are not filled with C3M; the viewer fetches them again later.
- **Checking**: On the 29 Tokyo tiles whose decoding by Apple itself (exported from the iOS 27 simulator) is published, the mesh triangles, positions, UV numbers and UV values, and the splat vertices, colors and attributes all match bit for bit
- **Where the format comes from**: Apple doesn't document it. It was worked out from the data, using the prose of [nmt3325/apple-maps-r3d-research](https://github.com/nmt3325/apple-maps-r3d-research) as clues (its code was not read, since it has no license). The reference data used for checking is from the same repository
- **Lane filling**: Decided by the mark in J (on about 150,000 splat lanes in the local tiles, the mark and the filling worked out from the data agree every time). For lanes without a mark: where the prediction is right the bit is 0, so with the right filling a literal never equals the prediction, and that is how it is chosen
- **Mesh field section**: int32 values for the face count, the corner pairs (va, vb) glued in advance, the vertex count and so on. The fields are laid out as 4 bytes each, and the byte string is packed with a table lookup, using each field's 4-bit mark (which bytes are written) as the bitmap (the same as mode 4 of the origin packing: an unwritten byte is table[previous byte], and each written byte sets table[previous] = it). When the section starts with 0C, that is packed again with a repeat bitmap. Every field in the local tiles reads correctly this way
- **Header numbers and small tiles**: The two numbers in the header and the two numbers after XYZ take 1 byte when small. They are all sizes of working space for decoding: a count n turned into "n if under 64, otherwise n + (floor(n / 64) + 1) × 64". The first header number comes from the triangle count and the two after XYZ from the UV count (all 2,487 surveyed tiles match; the second one after XYZ is occasionally a little larger). The second header number is the same formula applied to the byte length of the C bits (triangle count / 8, rounded up) plus the byte length of the non-C kinds (non-C triangle count / 4, rounded up) (all of another 2,489 tiles match). They are not used for reading. In small tiles the seam block, and in large low-zoom tiles the per-triangle C bits, are stored unpacked or as a 1-lane BYET
- **Version 1 container**: Rare in upper height slabs. It has no part and item framing: from byte 16 come 12 doubles for the rotation and translation, then the splats (starting with `D1 22 C9 07`), the mesh (starting with `E1 21 66 24`) and the image HEIC. The contents have the same form as version 2 and read the same way
- **Choosing the texture**: The "per-chart number" block of the mesh section gives the texture index for each UV chart (a group bounded by seams, numbered in traversal order). It is a list of int32: stored as is when small, otherwise as a 4-lane BYET (the value is lane 0, or lane 1 when lane 0 is absent). It is empty when there is one texture. UV values are in units of a quarter pixel of that chart's texture. On Fukuoka tiles this matches the material split of the C3M for the same place, down to the triangle counts (before, only the last texture was read, so such tiles had their images pasted wrongly)
- **Bands and planes at the edge of coverage**: A texture whose image entry has item 13 (value 1 or 2) joins the Flyover to the flat map at the edge of coverage, like mesh kinds 4 (edge band) and 3 (flat plane) of the old C3M. Value 1 is a band along the terrain with a coarse photo whose parts without imagery are uniform grey. Value 2 is a flat plane, often a pure black 16 px square image (near 0 m height in Paris). They are in z13–17 tiles at coastal and inland edges of coverage (such as west of Kameoka). As with the old C3M, they are left out of the glb (otherwise a grey band and black planes appear at the edge of coverage). They were first misread as water fills and tiled (on 6,000 local tiles their UVs never go outside 0–1). The extra block after the XYZ block (a 1-bit mark per vertex) marks the lower vertices of a sloping band that runs 25–57 m outward and 66.6 m down from the edge of the sea or a lake (every marked vertex is on the mesh boundary, belongs only to water-texture triangles, and sits at one shared height). The band is a slope with the water texture, apparently hiding the gap at the border with the neighboring ground. What the mark is used for (hiding the lower edge, or water depth) is unknown
- **Seams with the same UVs on both sides**: These occur only in coastal z13–14 tiles (4 of 2,487 surveyed), always on edges between land-photo triangles. They are short lines of 1–2 edges, with one end on the coastline (the mesh boundary) and the other stopping inland. They are lines where the surface was cut open to make the photo UVs, but since the photo is applied from straight above, both sides apparently ended up with the same UVs. UV numbering splits them like ordinary seams, but UV value prediction crosses them
- **How much can be read**: All 37,542 cached tiles can be read. The last 12 unreadable tiles (coastal z13–14) came from following the wrong order when predicting UV values. Coastal tiles have edges that carry a seam mark although both sides have the same UV numbers. UV value prediction crosses an edge when both sides have the same UV numbers, not by the mark (stopping at the mark shifts the prediction references, and the photo UVs go far out of range). After the fix, the photo is applied the same way as in the C3M of the same place. Coastal tiles of the same layout that could already be read, but whose photo UVs stuck out a little, had the same misreading
- **Drifting positions and large tiles**: In position prediction, the midpoint of the first triangle of a piece also becomes the previous prediction (it did not before). Positions drifted after pieces with only 3 vertices: 9 tiles could not be read, and 153 were read partly shifted. After the fix, tiles that carry over the old Flyover's shapes match the C3M in both positions and UVs within the quantization step. Also, the chart count (a small number) takes 3 bytes from 8,192 up (the top bit of the second byte marks the continuation). This made the remaining 3 tiles readable
- **The 4 splat values**: all in steps of 0.05. Compared against the shape, color and surroundings of splats in the cached tiles (about 600,000 for the normals, about 6 million for the per-zoom comparison)
  - Values 2 and 3 are an octahedral-packed normal. It matches the triangle's face normal (median dot product 0.996, within the quantization). 13% point to the back of the face, mostly toward the open side
  - Value 4 (0–3.15, piling up at step 63) is how much sun the splat gets (near π in the sun, 0 in shadow). It is the same quantity as the grey image attached to the mesh texture (item 6 below). Casting shadows over a height map built from the mesh and splat heights, with the sun direction varied in 15° steps, matches the value best at azimuth 150° (south-southeast), elevation 35° around Tokyo Station and at azimuth 195°, elevation 45° around Nagoya Station (correlations 0.38 and 0.37), and in both places that is the same direction that best matches the item 6 image of the same area. This is why it is large on the outside and top of trees, 0 inside and underneath, and larger for brighter colors (correlation 0.3–0.4). It was earlier taken for occlusion (how much sky is seen), but it matches sky visibility only weakly (rank correlation 0.29). Darkening colors by it does not get closer to Apple's screenshots (the colors already include the shading; Apple apparently uses it when redrawing with a different light direction)
  - Value 1 (0.05–1) is mostly around 0.7 and piles up at 1. It barely relates to shape, color or surroundings (correlations under 0.2) and is somewhat similar between neighbors (0.42). It is larger at low zooms, where splats are big (the share at 1 is 5% at z20 and 24% at z13). It seems to correspond to the other grey image of the mesh (item 8): both have a bell-shaped distribution and relate neither to brightness nor to the sun value. Item 8 is larger on horizontal faces (mean 0.60 on roofs and ground, 0.35 on walls) and small at windows, so it looks like a surface-quality (roughness-like) value, but this is not confirmed. Used as an edge-softness exponent, opacity or brightness, it moves away from Apple's screenshots
- **Index (C3MM version 2, style 100)**: An index says which height slabs of which tiles have data. Requesting `?style=100&v=<version>&x&y&z` with a zoom 9 tile returns the index for zooms 13–16, and with a zoom 13 tile the index for zooms 17–20 (no region). The reader is `flyover/c3mm.py`
  - **Container**: `C3MM`, version 2, the number of sections. Each section has a kind, a length, a compressed flag and the original length, and its body is LZMA (after a header of `5D 00 00 10 00` and 8 `FF` bytes)
  - **Tree**: For each root (the tiles of that zoom: x, y, height slab), the nodes follow depth-first. A node is 16 bits, 2 bits for each of 8 children (the low byte is the 4 in the lower half of the height, the high byte the 4 in the upper half). The low bit means "this child has data", the high bit "near the edge of the covered area" (a band about 2 tiles wide, plus the tile just outside)
  - **Height slabs form an octree**: A child's height slab is parent × 2 + (1 if in the upper half). This is why slabs continue at 8–15 above 0–3
  - **Height ranges**: Each root stores its lowest and highest point (ellipsoidal height) in 8 bits each, and each child stores, in 4 bits each, how far to narrow the parent's range from below and above in steps of 1/16 of that range (the actual heights of 148 out of 150 local tiles fit within 0.5 m)
  - **Check**: Compared with the "present / absent" state of 10,710 tiles fetched earlier. Every slab that disagreed was asked from the server again (102 slabs, after widening the comparison to 11,455 tiles). 90 were stale caches from an older version and 12 were slabs left on the server that the index does not list (in Toyama, h = 2 and 3 hold shapes overlapping the listed slabs at the same height; Apple's app follows the index and does not use them). The index was never the one that was wrong
  - **Use**: The server and the exporter request only the slabs in the index (`fetch_r3d` in `flyover/earth.py`; for 8 tiles at Nagoya Station, requests to Apple went from 56 to 22). When the index cannot be fetched, slabs are probed in order as before
  - **Section 9 (data generation)**: 1 or 2 per root. Tiles under a root with 1 are repackaged old Flyover shapes, with no splats and no lighting images (all 1,500 checked; Paris, for example). 2 is new data. Some indexes have no section 9
  - **Section 8 (data batch number)**: A number per root (97–944 seen). Neighboring roots share a number, and one index mixes several. The largest number equals the current manifest version (944), but it is not a version number (tiles that existed at version 731 and have not changed carry 891; requesting with that number as `v` gives 410)
  - **Unknown**: How the section 8 numbers are assigned, section 4 (5 bytes `02 00 10 0D 14`, only in the zoom 9 index; it reads as two index levels, one down to zoom 16 and one per zoom 13 tile down to zoom 20, but every index has the same value so this cannot be checked), and what the edge mark is used for
- **Grey images next to the texture**: Besides the texture (item 1), the image section holds two grey images at half the width and height (items 6 and 8), `03` (item 3) and 12 bytes (item 5: an f32 of 3.139 or 3.141 and two zeros)
  - **Item 6 is a map of sun and shadow**: a nearly black-and-white image, white in the sun. Casting shadows over a height map built from the mesh, with the sun direction varied, gives a correlation of 0.80 at azimuth 150°, elevation 35° around Tokyo Station and 0.79 at azimuth 195°, elevation 45° around Nagoya Station (−0.2 for directions that do not fit). Around the Empire State Building in New York it is 0.61 at azimuth 135°, elevation 55° (splat value 4 also fits best one step away, at azimuth 120° and the same elevation). Its correlation with the photo's brightness is 0.7–0.8. Multiplied by the item 5 number (close to π) it spans the same range as splat value 4 (0–π), and it fits best at the same sun direction, so it is the same quantity
  - **Item 8**: unrelated to brightness and to the sun map (correlations under 0.1), dark at windows and bright on horizontal faces (correlation with how much the face points up: 0.49). It does not match the amount of light from the sky (0.06). It seems to correspond to splat value 1 (surface quality? not confirmed)
  - **Items 14 and 15 of edge bands**: A band texture (one whose item 13 is 1) carries two 256 px square black-and-white images. Item 14 is a sharp mask that is white where the coarse photo has imagery, and item 15 is the same blurred (apparently to blend only the part with imagery into the map; unused here since bands are not drawn)
  - All of these look like material for relighting. The viewer's `?light=` uses item 6 (see "Changing the lighting" below); item 8 is not used
- **Changing the lighting (`?light=`)**: The server turns item 6 into a grey JPEG in the glb material's occlusionTexture and puts splat value 4 (the sun value) into the low 6 bits of the 16-bit opacity (`flyover/glb.py`). The viewer brightens the shadows to remove them and lights the result again with a different sky and sun (`morning`, `evening`, `cloudy`, `night`). Shadows in the photos are about R 0.10, G 0.15, B 0.17 of the sunlit brightness (linear values, median of 67 photos; bluish because only the blue sky lights them). Face directions come from the change of position across the screen. Shadows cast by buildings are not created anew. `check` shows item 6 as it is
- **Regions registered by name**: Of the regions in the height-slab list, 53 (Paris, Seattle, Madrid, Milan and others) are named after places instead of `Reg_z9_X_Y`, and the viewer used to leave them out (it never requested their tiles). They are now assigned to the zoom 9 tiles their center and radius touch (225 tiles) and covered (`flyover/client.py`). Both the old and the new Flyover can be fetched there. Also, the `region` of the new Flyover (style 98) only has to be present; its value does not matter (0 returns the same tile)
- **JPEG textures**: In regions where the old Flyover shapes were repackaged (Paris, for example), the texture is a JPEG (512 px square) instead of HEIC. It starts with `FF D8 FF` right after the image item's header. The UV unit is the same as for HEIC (1 / (4 × image width or height)). All 217 height slabs at zooms 14–20 around the Eiffel Tower were read. These used to be rejected as "no texture" and filled in with C3M
- **Height map (style 99)**: The iOS 27 manifest also lists style 99, requested with zoom 14 tiles (`?style=99&v=<version>&x&y&z`; it responds only where the new Flyover exists and is empty elsewhere). It is a 512 px square 16-bit grey PNG holding the surface height of buildings and ground in 1 m steps (one pixel is about 4 m in Tokyo). The value is the height above the lowest point of that tile. Its difference from the mesh's ellipsoidal height is constant within a tile regardless of height (median 23.0 m in the Tokyo Station tile, median deviation 0.9 m) and differs per tile (Nagoya 32.7 m, New York −37.4 m, Denver 1,567 m). The base height is in 12 bytes appended after the end of the PNG (IEND): an f32 base height (ellipsoidal; Tokyo Station 22.06 m, Nagoya 31.73 m, New York −38.28 m, Denver 1,566.50 m), an f32 of 1.0 (height per unit value), `10 01` (16 bits, 1 channel) and a u16 of 12 (the length of this trailer). Height = base + value, which is 0.5–1 m below the mesh height (in all 6 tiles checked). What it is used for is unknown (the view does not use it)
- **What is unknown**: The true meaning of splat value 1 and the item 8 image, and how Apple uses value 4 (the sun value) when drawing (with the 3 Apple screenshots at hand, not using it matches best; the view uses none of the 4 values now; checking further needs screenshots at different times of day). What the mark on the lower vertices of the water band is used for
